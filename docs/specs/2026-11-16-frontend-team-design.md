## 1. Why

The engine has a `frontend` team (`packages/engine/src/team.ts`, `TEAMS.frontend`) with the lead `lead-frontend`, one member, `review-ui-ux`, one gate, `ui-ux`, a seat by interface paths (`mandatory: { kind: "paths", interface: true }`) and `validates: true`, and a pack, `packs/frontend`, with three checklists and two knowledge files, beside the stack packs `packs/nuxt` and `packs/next`. ### The ten kinds of project

The user decided ten kinds, each with its playbook, its metric and its evidence, declared with the direction before the first file (section 5, rule 10). The metrics are the ones the research's notes name for each context (`conversion_evidence_by_context.md`), and the strength of the evidence is the second round's reading (`context_playbooks_evidence_round_two.md`), whose corrections win over the first round. "Opened" means the research read the page itself on 2026-11-16.

- Info-product sales page. Metric: conversion rate per unique visitor, then revenue per visitor and refund rate. Evidence: lore. The published experiments are three MarketingExperiments tests of 2004 on AdWords traffic, whose authors say the periods were too short and in one of which both versions lost money ("Long Copy vs. Short Copy", opened, https://marketingexperiments.com/?p=22175); the rest is vendor case studies and a vendor's observational benchmark, Unbounce's "Conversion Benchmark Report" of 2024 (opened, https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/), where the traffic channel dominates the benchmark. NextAfter's experiments on donation pages show copy length and video cutting both ways (opened, https://www.nextafter.com/research/).
- E-commerce. Metric: conversion rate per session, revenue per visitor, add-to-cart rate, cart abandonment and average order value. Evidence: strong for checkout, as usability findings and one meta-analysis. Baymard's "50 Cart Abandonment Rate Statistics" (opened, https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate) and "The Current State of Checkout UX" (opened, https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux) give failure rates across benchmarked sites, which are usability findings and not measured lifts. Browne and Swarbrick Jones, "What works in e-commerce - a meta-analysis of 6700 online experiments" (Qubit, 2017; opened as a PDF, https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2017-browne.pdf), a personalisation vendor's clients' data with a method assured by PwC, gives the mean uplift on revenue per visitor: scarcity +2.9%, social proof +2.3%, urgency +1.5%, abandonment recovery +1.1%, colour 0.0% and buttons -0.2%; about 2,600 of the 6,700 experiments were categorised.
- SaaS marketing and pricing site. Metric: visitor-to-trial, trial-to-paid and visitor-to-paid, or the demo request. Evidence: single tests and lore by the second round's reading, and the layout of a pricing page a hypothesis. In two rounds the research found no published experiment or meta-analysis on pricing-page layout (number of plans, decoy, default annual toggle), and every trial figure it holds rests on search summaries that disagree with each other.
- Product UI (web apps, dashboards). Metric: activation, time to value, feature adoption and retention, never page conversion. Evidence: strong in method, weak in transfer. Kohavi and others, "Seven Rules of Thumb for Web Site Experimenters" (KDD 2014; opened as a PDF, https://www.exp-platform.com/Documents/2014%20experimentersRulesOfThumb.pdf), reports controlled results with their replications and says that at Bing most experiments fail and "those that succeed improve key metrics by 0.1% to 1.0%, once diluted to overall impact"; Duolingo's delayed sign-up, "about a 20% increase in DAUs", is one company's account in an interview, with no sample sizes or intervals given (opened, https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker).
- Institutional and public sector. Metric: task completion (finding the information, completing the service). Evidence: standards built on qualitative research, with no measured lift. GOV.UK's "One thing per page" (opened, https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2015/07/03/one-thing-per-page/) rests on lab research, and its author says there is no quantitative data to share.
- Donations and non-profit. Metric: donation conversion, the one metric the research's note names for non-profits; average gift and recurring donor conversion are what its experiments and M+R's benchmark report. Evidence: strong, an experiment library. NextAfter's research library held 3,934 experiments on 2026-11-16, each with its rates, its sample against the sample required and a confidence level; "M+R Benchmarks 2026: Website performance" (opened, https://mrbenchmarks.com/website-performance/) gives mobile 52% of traffic and desktop 72% of revenue.
- Brand, portfolio, agency and campaign. Metric: the enquiry or contact for an agency or a portfolio; recall and engagement for a brand or a campaign. Evidence: hypothesis. After two rounds the research found no published experiment and no independent benchmark for this kind, and it says that awards, time on site and scroll depth are not outcomes.
- Content and media. Metric: page views per session, ad revenue, registration and paid subscription. Evidence: observational, from vendors. Google's "The business impact of Core Web Vitals" (opened, https://web.dev/case-studies/vitals-business-impact) collects publishers' own case studies of speed and layout stability; Piano's subscription benchmark is known to the research by a search result alone (https://piano.io/?p=12245 (not opened)).
- Mobile app. Metric: download-to-trial, trial-to-paid, download-to-paid by day 35, revenue per install, renewal and retention. Evidence: strong for paywalls, as one vendor's observational benchmark. RevenueCat's "The State of Subscription Apps in 10 minutes: lessons, trends, and benchmarks for 2026" (opened, https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026), over more than 115,000 apps: a hard paywall against freemium, 10.7% against 2.1% median conversion to paid by day 35, a metric the article labels "download-to-paid" in a chart caption and "trial-to-paid" in the text, with retention after one year on yearly plans the same, 27% against 28%.
- B2B lead generation. Metric: the qualified lead; visitor-to-lead, lead-to-opportunity and opportunity-to-close. Evidence: single tests and lore, with one base rate. Mida's "Do form A/B tests work? 50+ tests measured" (a vendor's own platform; opened, https://www.mida.so/change-types/form): 12% of the form tests won, 70% showed no measurable change and 17% lost, at a median traffic of 1,420 visitors per variant, so only large effects could be seen, and the page warns that a shorter form can lower the quality of the leads.

A project that fits none declares a new kind with its metric. Across kinds the research gives the base rate a playbook starts from: in Georgi Georgiev's "What Can Be Learned From 1,001 A/B Tests?" (opened, https://blog.analytics-toolkit.com/2022/what-can-be-learned-from-1001-a-b-tests/) the median lift is 0.08% and 33.5% of the tests had a significant result for the variant, the tests being those of one statistics platform's users, so one published win from another site is weak ground for a rule. Marketplaces and booking, a context of the research's first round, is not among the ten kinds decided.

### Proof with a label

The promise the user chose is "proof with a label". Every design decision carries one of five labels: norm, platform guidance, published study with its replication note, measured on this site, or hypothesis. A label never upgrades without a measurement, and a vendor's benchmark or case study gets no label of its own: it is said as the vendor's figure and carries the label "hypothesis" until measured on this site. The five labels are the user's decision of 2026-11-16; these two points are the session's reading from the research, told to the user the same day with the points taken under the standing rules. A hypothesis becomes "measured on this site" when the site measures it, and by nothing else.

The research is why the labels are needed. Few craft numbers are norms: the normative ones are WCAG 2.2's (4.5:1 and 3:1 for text, 3:1 for interface parts, a target of 24 CSS px), many are a platform's guidance (Apple's 44 pt and Android's 48 dp), and 45 to 75 characters a line, the 8-point grid and 60-30-10 are conventions with no study behind them that the research found (`visual_craft_rules_with_numbers.md`). The golden ratio fails as a preferred proportion: Höge's "The Golden Section Hypothesis—Its Last Funeral" (1997), read in its abstract, concludes that "the golden section hypothesis is a myth" (`primary_sources_verification_register.md`, section 1). Motion durations are convention, and the research found no study that measures the effect of interface animation on conversion (`motion_and_animation.md`). Section 3 gives each subject's sources with what each one is.

### What the team never claims and never builds

The user's context asked for work "baseado na neurociencia" and for what "viraliza mais" and "vicia mais"; the research answered, and the user then chose the promise above. The team never invokes dopamine, neuroscience or addiction. The grounds, from `perception_behaviour_and_engagement_evidence.md`:

- Neuroscience: the research found a small literature that predicts market outcomes for songs, advertisements and crowdfunding from the brain activity of a few dozen people, with no demonstrated value for interface decisions, and it classes the "dopamine hit" account of a like or a notification as lore. Its dopamine papers were recalled and not opened, and the register did not check them, so the team cites none of their figures.
- Addiction: the Hook model has no controlled test that the research found, and the Commission's preliminary findings under the Digital Services Act against TikTok (2026-02-06, the release read in full) and against Meta (2026-07-10, the release's lead text read) name "infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications" and the personalised recommender system as addictive design, TikTok's release adding that the findings "do not prejudge the outcome of the investigation". Streak rewards are not in those findings: they stand in Article 17 of the Digital Fairness Act proposal, as "features incentivising engagement at regular times or with greater frequency".
- Virality: it cannot be promised. Goel and others, "The Structural Virality of Online Diffusion" (Management Science, 2016; the author's PDF opened, https://5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf), over "a billion diffusion events on Twitter", find that "'viral hits' appear at a rate closer to one in a million" and that popularity is driven mostly by the largest broadcast. What a product can design for is a sharing feature: Aral and Walker, "Creating Social Contagion Through Viral Product Design" (Management Science, 2011), read in its abstract, a randomised field experiment over 9,687 users.

What the team uses is what works and is lawful: social proof, scarcity and urgency only when true, and defaults that serve the user. It never builds a deceptive pattern. The law, as the research read it at the official texts through the Publications Office (`primary_sources_verification_register.md`, section 3):

- Directive 2005/29/EC on unfair commercial practices, consolidated on 2022-05-28 (http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/02005L0029-20220528), Annex I, practices unfair in all circumstances. Point 7: "Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a very limited time", which is the fake timer and the fake stock claim. Point 23b: stating that reviews come from consumers who used or bought the product "without taking reasonable and proportionate steps to check that they originate from such consumers". Point 23c: submitting or commissioning false reviews. Point 11a: search results that do not disclose paid ranking.
- Directive 98/6/EC on price indication, consolidated on 2022-05-28 (http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/01998L0006-20220528), Article 6a, added by Directive (EU) 2019/2161: an announced price reduction shows the prior price, the lowest applied "during a period of time not shorter than 30 days" before it. In Portugal, Decreto-Lei n.º 109-G/2021, in force since 2022-05-28.
- Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, the Digital Services Act (http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32022R2065), Article 25: providers of online platforms do not design an interface that "deceives or manipulates", repeated prompts and a cancellation harder than the subscription being named in paragraph 3 as practices on which the Commission may issue guidelines; by paragraph 2 the prohibition does not apply to practices covered by Directive 2005/29/EC or Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
- Directive (EU) 2019/882, the European Accessibility Act (http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32019L0882): services provided after 28 June 2025, "e-commerce services" among them, are in scope, a microenterprise that provides services being exempt; in Portugal, Decreto-Lei n.º 82/2022.
- The Digital Fairness Act is a proposal and not law: COM(2026) 1100 final, 2026/0356 (COD), adopted by the Commission on 2026-11-11 (https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/digital-fairness-act_en), which would repeal Directive 2005/29/EC and whose Annex I, Part III, lists dark patterns. The team builds to the law in force and reads the proposal as the direction.
